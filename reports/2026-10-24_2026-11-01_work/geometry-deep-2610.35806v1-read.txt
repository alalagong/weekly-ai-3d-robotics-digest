[] Does VGGT Know What It Does Not Know?

[abstract1.1] Feed-forward 3D reconstruction models such as VGGT regress cameras and geometry in a single forward pass, but they report no uncertainty for the camera poses they predict: an incorrect relative pose looks no different from a correct one. We ask whether the frozen model nonetheless knows when its pose is wrong. On HardMatch, a benchmark of 1,000 deliberately difficult image pairs on which VGGT fails on 35% of pairs, a linear probe on VGGT’s frozen features predicts pose failure on held-out categories with ROC-AUC 93.5%, compared with 79.7% for VGGT’s own point-map confidence and 77.7% for a classifier trained on all of VGGT’s outputs. The probe catches failures VGGT is confident about, lets a downstream system abstain on the pairs it distrusts, and transfers without retraining to rotation failures on PhotoTourism and ScanNet-1500, beyond what co-visibility explains. This knowledge is absent from the per-image encoder: it is computed partway through the layers that fuse the two views, and the same holds for a second model, π3\pi^{3}. In essence, we find that VGGT knows more about its own failures than it reports.

[S1] 1 Introduction

[S1.p1.1] Feed-forward reconstruction models [25, 14, 23, 24, 26, 15, 12] predict cameras, depth and point maps from a set of images in a single forward pass, replacing much of the classical Structure-from-Motion (SfM) [7] pipeline. VGGT [23] is the most widely used of these models. It outputs a per-pixel confidence for depth and point maps, but no uncertainty for the camera poses it regresses. Downstream systems, such as SLAM, mapping, novel-view synthesis, therefore cannot distinguish a reliable relative pose from a badly wrong one.

[S1.p2.1] Two observations make this gap worth studying. First, these models do fail: on HardMatch [18], a recent benchmark of hand-annotated, deliberately difficult image pairs, VGGT’s predicted geometry is grossly wrong (median epipolar error above 50 px) on 35% of pairs. Second, the large-language-model literature has shown that internal representations often encode whether a model’s own output is correct [11, 4, 2], even when the model’s stated confidence does not. This motivates the question in our title: does a feed-forward 3D model know when its pose is wrong, and is that knowledge linearly readable from its frozen features?

[S1.p3.1] We answer it with linear probes [1, 3] on frozen VGGT activations at every block, evaluated with category-grouped cross-validation so that every test category is unseen during training, and compared against everything VGGT itself outputs. The main contributions are as follows:

[S1.I1.i1.p1.1] We show that VGGT’s frozen features encode its own pose failures far more clearly than anything it outputs (ROC-AUC 93.5%93.5\% vs. 79.7%79.7\% for its own confidence on HardMatch; Fig. 1), including failures it is confident about.

[S1.I1.i2.p1.1] We locate where this knowledge forms: it is absent from the per-image encoder and emerges partway through the layers that fuse the views, in VGGT and in a second model, π3\pi^{3}.

[S1.I1.i3.p1.1] We show that the signal is useful beyond the benchmark it was trained on: it supports selective prediction and transfers without retraining to rotation failures on PhotoTourism and ScanNet-1500.

[S2] 2 Related Work

[S2.SS0.SSS0.Px1] Feed-forward 3D reconstruction.

[S2.SS0.SSS0.Px1.p1.1] DUSt3R [25] regresses aligned point maps for an image pair and MASt3R [14] adds dense matching. VGGT [23] predicts cameras, depth, point maps and tracks for many views in one pass through alternating frame-wise and global attention; VGGT-Ω\Omega [24], π3\pi^{3} [26], Depth Anything 3 [15] and MapAnything [12] extend the recipe. These models expose per-pixel confidence for geometry, learned with an uncertainty-weighted loss, but no uncertainty for camera poses.

[S2.SS0.SSS0.Px2] Uncertainty of feed-forward reconstruction.

[S2.SS0.SSS0.Px2.p1.1] Hillemann et al. [9] evaluate how well VGGT’s per-pixel confidence tracks depth and point errors on DTU and note that VGGT provides no pose uncertainty. ReVar3R [16] estimates per-point uncertainty by perturbing the inputs of a frozen model. Both target per-point geometry; we target the correctness of the predicted relative pose, and we read it from internal features rather than outputs or input perturbations.

[S2.SS0.SSS0.Px3] What 3D foundation models encode.

[S2.SS0.SSS0.Px3.p1.1] Zhang et al. [27] find an emergent ability to reason about non-overlapping views; Starý et al. [21] trace how relative pose forms across the layers of a DUSt3R variant; Nordström et al. [19] study what feed-forward 3D models know about image matching. Closest to us, Co-VGGT [28] probes VGGT’s layers for co-visibility. Co-visibility is a property of the scene; failure is a property of the prediction. HardMatch pairs are co-visible by construction, and on PhotoTourism our probe predicts failure beyond ground-truth co-visibility (Sec. 4.3).

[S2.SS0.SSS0.Px4] Probing, self-knowledge and failure prediction.

[S2.SS0.SSS0.Px4.p1.1] Linear probes measure what is linearly available in frozen features [1, 3]. For language models, internal states encode whether the model’s own answer is correct [11, 4, 2]. Failure prediction and selective prediction [8, 6, 13] typically train dedicated heads; we keep the model frozen and compare against the confidence it already emits.

[S3] 3 Method

[S3.SS0.SSS0.Px1] Model and inputs.

[S3.SS0.SSS0.Px1.p1.1] We use VGGT [23] (1B) frozen, with no fine-tuning. Each image pair is processed in one forward pass of two frames; we study pairs because HardMatch defines its ground truth, and its official evaluation, per pair, and test up to 1616 views in Sec. 4.3. Images are passed as RGB in [0,1][0,1] (VGGT normalises internally), resized so that the longer side is 518518 px with the shorter side a multiple of 1414, and centred on a square canvas. From the predicted pose encoding we decode extrinsics [𝐑i|𝐭i][\mathbf{R}_{i}|\mathbf{t}_{i}] and intrinsics 𝐊i\mathbf{K}_{i} for both frames. As a second model we use π3\pi^{3} [26] with the same inputs; it predicts camera-to-world poses but no intrinsics, so we fit a focal length by least squares to its local point maps. We hook its encoder blocks 66, 1212 and 2323, all 3636 decoder blocks and the camera head’s features.

[S3.SS1] 3.1 Defining failure

[S3.SS1.SSS0.Px1] Pairs without ground-truth poses (HardMatch).

[S3.SS1.SSS0.Px1.p1.1] HardMatch [18] provides, for each pair, a set of hand-annotated correspondences (median 1111 per pair) but no camera poses. Following the official protocol, which evaluates any method through the fundamental matrix it implies, we form VGGT’s own relative pose 𝐑=𝐑2​𝐑1⊤\mathbf{R}=\mathbf{R}_{2}\mathbf{R}_{1}^{\top}, 𝐭=𝐭2−𝐑𝐭1\mathbf{t}=\mathbf{t}_{2}-\mathbf{R}\mathbf{t}_{1} and fundamental matrix

[S3.E1]  | 𝐅=𝐇2⊤​𝐊2−⁣⊤​[𝐭]×​𝐑​𝐊1−1​𝐇1,\mathbf{F}=\mathbf{H}_{2}^{\top}\,\mathbf{K}_{2}^{-\top}[\mathbf{t}]_{\times}\mathbf{R}\,\mathbf{K}_{1}^{-1}\,\mathbf{H}_{1}, |  | (1)

[S3.SS1.SSS0.Px1.p1.2] where 𝐇i\mathbf{H}_{i} maps original-image pixels to the network’s input canvas. For every annotated correspondence we compute the symmetric epipolar distance [17], take the median over the pair, and call the pair a failure if it exceeds τ\tau. We use τ=50\tau=50 px (about 5%5\% of the image width) as the main threshold and report 1010, 2020 and 100100 px. As in the official code, we keep pairs with at least 88 correspondences and drop pairs labelled dynamic, leaving 903903 pairs in 9797 categories.

[S3.SS1.SSS0.Px2] Pairs with ground-truth poses (PhotoTourism, ScanNet-1500).

[S3.SS1.SSS0.Px2.p1.1] Here we compare VGGT’s relative pose with ground truth directly: the geodesic rotation error, the angle between translation directions (sign-agnostic, as in the ScanNet-1500 protocol [22]), and the pose error max⁡(eR,et)\max(e_{R},e_{t}).

[S3.SS2] 3.2 Probing for failure

[S3.SS2.SSS0.Px1] Features.

[S3.SS2.SSS0.Px1.p1.1] We hook seven depths of the frozen network: blocks 66, 1212 and 2323 of the DINOv2-initialised [20] ViT encoder, which processes each image independently; the output of the aggregator’s last frame-attention block and last global (cross-frame) attention block; the normalised camera token that enters the camera head; and the input of the camera head’s trunk at its final refinement iteration. For the layer analysis we additionally pool the output of every one of the 2424 encoder and 4848 aggregator blocks. Tokens are mean-pooled per image, and a pair is represented by the mean of its two image vectors.

[S3.SS2.SSS0.Px2] Probe and protocol.

[S3.SS2.SSS0.Px2.p1.1] A probe is an ℓ2\ell_{2}-regularised logistic regression on standardised features reduced to 128128 dimensions by PCA, with the regularisation strength chosen by an inner 55-fold cross-validation and balanced class weights. All in-domain numbers use 1010-fold cross-validation grouped by HardMatch category, so every test pair comes from a category the probe never saw. We report ROC-AUC with 95%95\% confidence intervals from a cluster bootstrap that resamples whole categories (1,0001{,}000 resamples). For transfer, a probe is trained on all HardMatch pairs and applied unchanged to the other datasets.

[S3.SS2.SSS0.Px3] Baselines from VGGT’s own outputs.

[S3.SS2.SSS0.Px3.p1.1] We compare against signals a user of VGGT already has, each with a direction fixed in advance: the lower of the two images’ mean point-map confidence, the lower mean depth confidence, image sharpness (variance of the Laplacian), the predicted relative rotation angle, and the ratio of predicted focal lengths. We also train classifiers, with the same cross-validation as the probes, on (i) all confidence statistics and (ii) everything VGGT outputs: confidence statistics, both extrinsics, focal lengths and relative rotation. Baseline (ii) asks whether the probe merely re-reads VGGT’s outputs.

[S4.SS2] 4.2 HardMatch: does VGGT encode its own failures?

[S4.SS2.p1.1] After the official filtering, HardMatch contains 903903 valid pairs in 9797 categories (830830 in the official test split), including large viewpoint changes, aerial-to-ground pairs, illumination and season changes, drawings and repeating structure. Judged by the geometry its own cameras imply, VGGT fails on 34.9%34.9\% of pairs at 5050 px (63.7%63.7\%, 51.5%51.5\% and 25.1%25.1\% at 1010, 2020 and 100100 px, respectively), and reaches mAA@10 px of 19.9%19.9\% on the official test split. [S4.SS2.SSS0.Px1] The features know more than the outputs.

[S4.SS2.SSS0.Px1.p1.1] Table 2 compares what a user of VGGT can already observe with what is linearly readable inside it. The most informative output is the point-map confidence (79.7%79.7\%), followed by depth confidence (66.5%66.5\%); the predicted pose itself is nearly uninformative (relative rotation 62.9%62.9\%, focal ratio 51.1%51.1\%). [S4.SS2.SSS0.Px1.p2.1] The gap has a plausible explanation. VGGT’s confidence is trained per pixel to down-weight hard-to-regress points; it describes how certain each depth or point is, not whether the two cameras are placed consistently. The fused features, by contrast, are the very representation from which the camera head regresses the relative pose, and they retain evidence that the two views were not registered consistently, which the camera head discards.

[S4.SS2.SSS0.Px2] Robustness.

[S4.SS2.SSS0.Px2.p1.1] Table 3 rules out the obvious artefacts. Shuffled labels give chance (51.6%51.6\%), so the grouped cross-validation does not leak; image size and aspect ratio alone give chance (49.8%49.8\%), so the padding of the input canvas does not leak the label. Pairs on which VGGT predicts an almost-zero baseline make the fundamental matrix ill-conditioned and could produce spurious failures, but the predicted baseline does not predict failure (49.9%49.9\%) and removing those pairs leaves the probe at 93.4%93.4\%. The advantage over confidence holds at every threshold, on the official test split, and for every pair representation and PCA size we tried. Combining layers does not help, and combining the three backbone blocks still gives only 64.1%64.1\%.

[S4.SS2.SSS0.Px3] Where the signal emerges.

[S4.SS2.SSS0.Px3.p1.1] To locate the signal we probe every block of the network (Fig. 3a). The per-image encoder never exceeds 65.2%65.2\%: what makes a pair hard is not visible in either image alone. More surprisingly, the first half of the aggregator adds nothing either, although it already alternates frame and global attention: through block 1010 the probe stays between 63%63\% and 66%66\%. It then rises abruptly, from 69%69\% after block 1111 to 81%81\% after block 1212, 88%88\% after block 1313 and 93%93\% after block 1515, and stays at 9393–94%94\% to the last block (peak 94.0%94.0\%, block 1818). The failure signal is therefore not a by-product of the two views meeting; it is computed in a narrow band of middle aggregator blocks. A training-free reading appears later still. The cosine similarity between the two views’ pooled features stays at the encoder’s level (6161–69%69\%) through block 1515 and then climbs to 83.7%83.7\% at the last block, above VGGT’s own confidence: in its final layers, VGGT’s representations of the two views stop agreeing when its pose is wrong. At the camera token this similarity falls back to 71.0%71.0\% while a trained probe stays at 93.2%93.2\%; the information is still present, but no longer as a simple disagreement.

[S4.SS2.SSS0.Px4] A second model: π3\pi^{3}.

[S4.SS2.SSS0.Px4.p1.1] π3\pi^{3} [26] also alternates frame-wise and global attention, but has no reference view and predicts camera-to-world poses and local point maps, from which we recover the focal length by least squares. We run it on the same pairs with the same failure definition. It fails on 34.6%34.6\% of them (mAA@10 18.4%18.4\% on the test split) and agrees with VGGT’s failure label on 81%81\%. Table 4 repeats the main comparison. π3\pi^{3}’s own confidence is a weaker failure signal than VGGT’s (62.2%62.2\%), and on its most confident half, where 27%27\% of pairs still fail, it is at chance (52.8%52.8\%). A probe on its last decoder block nonetheless reaches 91.7%91.7\%, against 76.9%76.9\% for a classifier trained on all of its outputs, and 92.7%92.7\% on that most confident half. The layer profile repeats as well (Fig. 3b): flat through the encoder and the first 2121 decoder blocks (≤67%\leq 67\%), then a steep rise to 92%92\% by block 3333. One difference is instructive: in π3\pi^{3} the training-free cosine never rises (≤69%\leq 69\%). Failure is linearly decodable in both models, but the simple reading that the two views stop agreeing is specific to VGGT.

[S4.SS2.SSS0.Px5] Confidently wrong predictions.

[S4.SS2.SSS0.Px5.p1.1] Confidence and failure are correlated at the level of the whole benchmark, but how much does confidence tell us once we already know it? We split the pairs into quartiles of point-map confidence. The failure rate falls from 64%64\% in the least confident quartile to 6%6\% in the most confident, yet within each quartile confidence ranks failures close to chance (Fig. 4) (AUC 47%47\%, 61%61\%, 64%64\%, 57%57\%) while the probe stays high (90%90\%, 91%91\%, 92%92\%, 82%82\%). On the most confident half of the pairs, where 12.2%12.2\% still fail, the probe reaches 90.0%90.0\% [85.485.4, 94.294.2] against 69.9%69.9\% [61.261.2, 77.877.8]. These are the failures a downstream system cannot see. Figure 2 shows examples: an aerial-to-ground pair, a large viewpoint change and a photograph matched to a drawing, all of which VGGT places confidently but wrongly and the probe flags, together with a correct pair on which confidence raises a false alarm and an extreme-zoom pair the probe misses.

[S4.SS2.SSS0.Px6] Per category.

[S4.SS2.SSS0.Px6.p1.1] Failure rates vary widely across HardMatch’s categories, from 27%27\% on building and texture pairs to 74%74\% on celestial ones (Fig. 5). The probe exceeds VGGT’s confidence in every category with enough pairs except doppelgängers, where both reach 92%92\%; the largest gains are on the categories where appearance changes most, namely celestial (82%82\% vs. 60%60\%), drawing (95%95\% vs. 77%77\%) and illumination (92%92\% vs. 71%71\%).

[S4.SS2.SSS0.Px7] Using the signal: selective prediction.

[S4.SS2.SSS0.Px7.p1.1] A system that can abstain on some pairs, for example by requesting another view or falling back to a slower pipeline, needs a ranking rather than a calibrated probability. Keeping the 50%50\% of pairs the probe trusts most yields a 3.8%3.8\% failure rate and mAA@10 of 34.9%34.9\%; ranking by VGGT’s confidence yields 12.4%12.4\% and 29.7%29.7\%, and a perfect oracle 0%0\% and 38.3%38.3\% (Fig. 6). Over all coverages, the area under the risk–coverage curve is 0.0960.096 for the probe, 0.1540.154 for confidence, 0.1690.169 for all outputs and 0.0700.070 for the oracle, so the probe closes about 70%70\% of the gap between VGGT’s confidence and a perfect ranking.

[S4.SS2.SSS0.Px8] A negative result: routing to a matcher.

[S4.SS2.SSS0.Px8.p1.1] We also tried to route each pair either to VGGT or to the dedicated matcher LoMa [18]. On HardMatch, LoMa (mAA@10 50.8%50.8\% on the test split) almost never does worse than VGGT (19.9%19.9\%): even an oracle that always picks the better of the two reaches only 52.1%52.1\%, and a learned router that uses the probe reaches 51.1%51.1\%. With so little complementarity there is nothing for the probe to exploit, and we make no routing claim.

[S4.SS3] 4.3 PhotoTourism: transfer to rotation failures

[S4.SS3.p1.1] HardMatch defines failure through epipolar error, which mixes rotation, translation and intrinsics errors, and its probe is trained on HardMatch’s own categories. PhotoTourism [10] lets us test both limitations at once: its poses come from large-scale structure-from-motion, so failure can be defined on the rotation alone, and none of its images were used to train the probe. We sample 1,2621{,}262 pairs from 1616 landmarks, stratified into four bins of ground-truth co-visibility, and apply the HardMatch probe unchanged.

[S4.SS3.SSS0.Px1] VGGT rarely fails on landmark photographs.

[S4.SS3.SSS0.Px1.p1.1] Rotation errors above 15∘15^{\circ} occur on only 1.7%1.7\% of the pairs, and almost all of them on pairs that barely overlap: 6.6%6.6\% of pairs with co-visibility below 0.020.02 fail, against at most 0.3%0.3\% in every other bin (Tab. 5, top). At the looser threshold of 5∘5^{\circ}, 6.8%6.8\% of pairs fail. These pairs are much easier than HardMatch, which is why we use PhotoTourism as a transfer test rather than as a training set.

[S4.SS3.SSS0.Px2] The HardMatch probe transfers.

[S4.SS3.SSS0.Px2.p1.1] Without seeing a single PhotoTourism image, the probe predicts rotation failures with AUC 94.6%94.6\% at 10∘10^{\circ} and 97.5%97.5\% at 15∘15^{\circ} (87.1%87.1\% at 5∘5^{\circ}), above VGGT’s confidence at every threshold and on par with a probe trained on PhotoTourism itself (Tab. 5, bottom). The score is also related to the size of the error, not just to a threshold: over all 1,2621{,}262 pairs its Spearman correlation with the log rotation error is 0.430.43, against 0.330.33 for confidence. The intervals are wide because there are few failures (2525 at 10∘10^{\circ}), but the ordering is the same at every threshold.

[S4.SS3.SSS0.Px3] It is not just co-visibility.

[S4.SS3.SSS0.Px3.p1.1] Because the failures concentrate on barely overlapping pairs, a probe that merely detected low overlap, as Co-VGGT’s [28] does by design, would also score well. Three observations argue against this explanation. First, ground-truth co-visibility itself, which a deployed system would not have, predicts the failures less well than the probe (87.0%87.0\% and 89.8%89.8\%). Second, among only the barely overlapping pairs, where co-visibility is nearly constant, the probe still separates failures from successes (86.9%86.9\% and 89.5%89.5\%) better than confidence (79%79\%) and co-visibility (7474–76%76\%). Third, with co-visibility partialled out, the probe’s rank correlation with the rotation error remains 0.230.23, twice that of confidence (0.110.11). The probe detects whether VGGT got the rotation wrong, not merely whether the views overlap.

[S4.SS3.SSS0.Px4] Multi-view graphs: no downstream gain.

[S4.SS3.SSS0.Px4.p1.1] We also built ten 1616-image graphs from PhotoTourism scenes and ran VGGT on all 120120 pairs of each. On these 1,2001{,}200 edges the HardMatch probe again separates rotation failures (eR>15∘e_{R}>15^{\circ}, 1.75%1.75\% of edges) better than confidence (98.6%98.6\% vs. 92.7%92.7\%). We then asked whether discarding distrusted edges improves rotation averaging. Robust averaging over all edges already reduces the mean absolute-rotation error from 1.27∘1.27^{\circ} (least squares) to 0.70∘0.70^{\circ}; dropping the 10%10\% of edges the probe distrusts most gives 0.66∘0.66^{\circ}, dropping by confidence 0.67∘0.67^{\circ}, and even an oracle that removes every edge whose true error exceeds 15∘15^{\circ} gives 0.67∘0.67^{\circ}. With fewer than 2%2\% of edges failing, robust averaging already absorbs them; showing a downstream benefit needs graphs with far denser failures than these.

[S4.SS3.SSS0.Px5.p1.1] HardMatch defines its ground truth per pair, so our main study uses pairs. To test whether this matters, we sample 9696 sets of 1616 images from the PhotoTourism scenes and run VGGT on each set as one 1616-view pass, as two 88-view passes, as four 44-view passes, and on every pair inside the 44-view groups, so that the same 2,3042{,}304 pairs are predicted with 22, 44, 88 and 1616 views (Tab. 6). The probe trained on two-view HardMatch pairs still ranks the remaining failures well above chance without retraining (7474–86%86\% for 44–1616 views), and VGGT’s own confidence does comparably (8282–93%93\%); with 2828–3535 failures per setting, neither is clearly better. Even with two views, confidence is ahead for small errors (above 5∘5^{\circ}: 91.2%91.2\% vs. 81.5%81.5\%) and the probe for larger ones (above 10∘10^{\circ}: 95.7%95.7\% vs. 93.6%93.6\%). Extra views thus mainly remove the failures the probe is meant to catch; failure awareness for many-view inputs needs a benchmark on which such inputs still fail.

[S4.SS4] 4.4 ScanNet-1500: failure modes differ

[S4.SS4.p1.1] ScanNet-1500 [5, 22] is the standard indoor benchmark for relative pose: 1,5001{,}500 pairs from 100100 rooms, with poses from RGB-D reconstruction. Indoor scenes add low texture, repeated structure and, unlike PhotoTourism, many pairs taken from nearby positions.

[S4.SS4.SSS0.Px1] VGGT’s indoor failures are translation failures.

[S4.SS4.SSS0.Px1.p1.1] VGGT’s rotations on ScanNet are accurate (median error 1.2∘1.2^{\circ}; only 2.1%2.1\% of pairs exceed 5∘5^{\circ}). Its pose errors come almost entirely from the direction of the translation: 113113 of the 115115 pairs with pose error above 15∘15^{\circ} have a rotation error below 5∘5^{\circ}. These failures are concentrated on short baselines (Tab. 7, top): the translation direction is wrong by more than 15∘15^{\circ} on 47%47\% of pairs whose cameras are less than 1010 cm apart, but on fewer than 1%1\% of pairs more than 4040 cm apart. This is a known property of two-view geometry rather than a peculiarity of VGGT: when the cameras barely move, the direction of their small displacement is weakly constrained by the images.

[S4.SS4.SSS0.Px2.p1.1] The few rotation failures behave differently: they occur at the longest baselines (3.3%3.3\% of pairs above 4040 cm, none below 2020 cm), the regime closest to HardMatch’s wide-baseline pairs. Yet a probe trained on ScanNet detects these failures with AUC 90.590.5–91.0%91.0\%. VGGT’s fused features therefore encode the short-baseline failures as well, but along a direction that a probe trained on HardMatch’s gross failures does not learn. Failure awareness is not a single scalar inside the model: one linear readout carries across datasets for wrong orientation, and ill-conditioned translation calls for its own.

[S5] 5 Limitations

[S5.p1.1] Two models. We study VGGT and π3\pi^{3}; larger or newer models (VGGT-Ω\Omega, MapAnything) may behave differently, and the training-free cosine reading already differs between the two. Labels. HardMatch has no ground-truth poses, so failure is defined through the epipolar error of about 1111 annotated correspondences per pair, which mixes rotation, translation and intrinsics errors; the ground-truth datasets confirm the result for rotation but have few failures (PhotoTourism: 2222 above 15∘15^{\circ}). Two views. We study image pairs, because HardMatch defines its ground truth per pair. With more views, failures on PhotoTourism become rare (0.3%0.3\% with 1616 views) and the two-view probe is no longer clearly better than VGGT’s confidence on them; filtering edges by the probe also did not improve rotation averaging (Sec. 4.3). Supervision. The probe is trained with failure labels, whereas VGGT’s confidence is free. We therefore compare against classifiers trained on VGGT’s outputs under the same protocol, and test the probe on data it was not trained on. Failure modes. A single probe does not cover short-baseline translation errors (Sec. 4.4).

[S6] 6 Conclusion

[S6.p1.1] VGGT knows more about its own failures than it reports. A linear readout of its frozen features predicts when its relative pose is wrong far better than its own confidence or anything else it outputs, including when it is confident; it supports selective prediction and transfers to rotation failures on other datasets. The signal emerges abruptly in the middle of the network, in both VGGT and π3\pi^{3}. Costing one linear layer on features the model already computes, it is a practical reliability signal for systems built on feed-forward reconstruction, and evidence that self-knowledge, studied extensively in language models, is also present in 3D foundation models.
