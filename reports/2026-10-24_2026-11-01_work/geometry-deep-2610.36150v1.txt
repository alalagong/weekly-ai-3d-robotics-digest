That assumption is inside the backbone too: the positional encoding and the training loss both bind an image position to a direction in space, and both depend on the lens. We condition both on the dense ray field, the unit direction through every pixel, which describes any central camera without a lens type, distortion coefficients or a bound on field of view. Angular coordinates give the rotary encoding the measured ray angle of each patch, evaluated per view, and solid-angle weighting scales the point loss by the area each pixel covers on the sphere, so neither the encoding nor the supervision depends on the lens. Any3R is therefore a feed-forward model that takes any central camera, and is robust to mixing many camera types per scene, with no retraining per lens. On a benchmark of thirteen central cameras across three datasets, where every method reconstructs with its own predicted camera poses, it is the most consistently accurate method. Any3R enables scalable 3D reconstruction from heterogeneous crowdsourced and archival imagery collections sourced across diverse camera types. Geometry foundation models such as VGGT [57], π3\pi^{3} [61], MapAnything [25], VGGT-Ω\Omega [58] and Depth Anything 3 [34] now solve it with a feed-forward network [60, 29, 13, 4, 56, 59, 65, 75]. A regular camera sees only a narrow part of a scene in each view, so covering a whole room or street takes many views captured densely and carefully. Figure 2: Motivation. Read from the pixel grid (ii), a patch position stands for a different direction and a pixel for a different solid angle on every lens (i), and the ray field (iii) corrects both. In the bottom row the ray field is withheld, so the backbone reads every frame as a panorama and the reconstruction follows that reading, which is correct only at 360∘360^{\circ}. The direct way to cover more of a scene per view is a wider lens, and wide-angle and panoramic cameras are already common in autonomous driving [72, 33] and indoor scanning [5, 1]. Feed-forward models cannot use such images as they are, because they read every frame as a pinhole image. Recent work adapts them to particular lenses, with a discrete token for the camera model, an adapter around a frozen backbone, a pairwise alignment stage, a conversion of the input to a cubemap, or retraining on equirectangular images [22, 12, 19, 3, 18, 31], but each supports only a fixed list of camera models, so an unseen camera needs retraining or a new conversion step. Mixing cameras of different types in one reconstruction, such as a pinhole view, a fisheye frame and a panorama of one scene as in Fig. 1, is harder still. Wid3R [22] and Fisheye3R [12] mix only the camera types they support, CAM3R [19] reconstructs two views at a time and aligns them afterwards, and the panoramic models accept panoramas alone. MapAnything [25] and OmniVGGT [39] take the intrinsics of each view as input and could mix cameras in principle, but a pinhole intrinsic matrix cannot describe a field of view of 180∘180^{\circ} or more, the range of many fisheye lenses and every panorama. Both limits trace back to the backbone’s positional encoding, which none of these methods changes: it locates each patch by its pixel index [49]. On a pinhole camera, the index tells the backbone roughly where the patch looks; on a fisheye, each pixel covers a wider angle, so the same index points somewhere else (Fig. 2(ii)). The training loss has a similar problem: it weights every pixel equally, but a fisheye pixel near the edge sees a different amount of the scene than one at the center (Fig. 2(i)). We present Any3R, which removes the pinhole assumption by giving the ray direction of every pixel to both the encoding and the loss (Fig. 2(iii)). In pinhole, fisheye and panoramic cameras, all rays pass through one point. Such central cameras are fully described by the ray direction of each pixel, whatever the lens. Angular coordinates position a patch by the angle of its ray instead of its index, so one unit of position means the same angle on every camera. Solid-angle weighting scales the loss by how much of the scene each pixel sees, so every part of the scene counts equally whatever the lens. Because each view carries its own ray field, Any3R reconstructs from any central camera without rectification, and views from different cameras can be processed together, in any number. On three public datasets imaged through thirteen camera models, where every method reconstructs with its own predicted camera poses, Any3R gives the most accurate reconstruction and camera poses on wide-angle and panoramic lenses and is on par with the strongest pinhole baseline at 90∘90^{\circ}. It also outperforms the methods built for wide-angle or panoramic lenses on every lens they take, and its pose estimates on real pinhole photographs are competitive with the strongest pinhole baselines. Its margin over the pinhole models is smallest at 90∘90^{\circ} and much larger on the wider lenses. Our contributions are threefold: • A feed-forward model that reconstructs from views of any mix of central cameras, with no calibration search, no undistortion and no retraining per lens. • Angular coordinates and solid-angle weighting, two modules that condition the positional encoding and the training loss on the dense ray field, so that one set of weights serves every central camera. • State-of-the-art reconstruction and camera pose on three public datasets imaged through thirteen central camera models, where every method reconstructs with its own predicted camera poses, with the largest margins on wide-angle and panoramic lenses. 2 Related Work Figure 3: Overview. The ray field 𝑹\bm{R} of each view gives its patches angular coordinates for the rotary encoding in intra-frame attention, and the heads predict the camera poses and a radial distance dd per pixel, decoded as 𝑿=d​𝑹\bm{X}=d\bm{R}. In training, the point loss ℒpt\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{pt}} compares 𝑿\bm{X} with the ground truth 𝑿gt\bm{X}^{\mathrm{gt}}, and the solid angle d​Ω\mathrm{d}\Omega of the same 𝑹\bm{R} weighs the error of each pixel, shown here on the panorama, where cells of equal pixel count cover unequal areas of the sphere. 2.1 3D Geometry Foundation Models Classical structure-from-motion recovers geometry by optimising correspondences and poses jointly [43], and per-scene neural representations [37, 26] keep that optimisation while changing what is optimised. DUSt3R [60] instead trained one network to regress a pointmap in a common frame, removing the optimisation loop and the need for known intrinsics, and MASt3R [29] added a matching head to the same backbone [62, 63, 11, 38]. Extending two views to many has taken several forms: a global stage on top of the pairwise backbone [13, 4], a latent state that lets views be streamed [56, 59], and set-level attention that fuses hundreds at once [65]. VGGT [57] predicts poses, depth, pointmaps and tracks together, π3\pi^{3} [61] removes the dependence on a reference view, VGGT-Ω\Omega [58] scales the recipe, and MonST3R [75] adapts it to moving scenes. Depth Anything 3 [34] reaches the same outputs from the monocular depth line [66, 67, 71, 40, 2, 24], and the same shift has occurred in SLAM [53] and in feed-forward radiance fields [20, 76, 6, 7, 68, 47]. MapAnything [25] and OmniVGGT [39] add optional conditioning on intrinsics, extrinsics or depth, which is the closest prior setting to ours. All of them assume pinhole input: the focal length may vary, the projection model may not. 2.2 3D Reconstruction for Different Camera Types A central camera is described by its ray field, parameterised compactly for rectilinear, fisheye and catadioptric optics [78, 23, 16, 36, 54, 42, 48]. Learning pipelines instead rectify the image to a rectilinear grid, which is lossy at wide field of view and impossible past 180∘180^{\circ}, so work on panoramic and fisheye input changes the operator instead: spherical convolutions [52, 10, 9, 15, 50], resampled charts [8, 14], monocular panoramic depth [80, 55, 21, 51, 41, 32, 44, 73] and multi-view fisheye depth [64, 27, 28, 72, 33]. Each fixes one projection, so a change of lens is a change of model. Recent feed-forward methods attach the camera at the backbone’s periphery, by retraining on one projection [18, 31], a token [22], an adapter [12], pairwise alignment [19] or a cubemap [3], or train on renderings through many lenses [30], but keep the pinhole meaning of a rotary coordinate [49] inside the attention, which is what a change of lens alters. RayTun3R [45] reaches the same diagnosis but adapts per sequence at test time. G-ray [77] parameterises the rotary phase by camera-local ray angles and evaluates it on pinhole and fisheye crops, while we mix eight central camera models from 90∘90^{\circ} to 360∘360^{\circ}. OmniPoint [69] encodes the ray field of the same three projection families from a single image, without relative pose or cross-view attention. Ray and Plücker coordinates have also served as geometric position [46, 74]. Reusing a pretrained rotary encoding adds a requirement that a freshly trained one lacks: wherever the pretrained coordinate already agrees with the ray angle, it must be reproduced exactly, or a valid prior is lost. 3 Method 3.1 Preliminary The input is a set of SS views 𝑽={(𝑰s,𝒞s)}s=1S\bm{V}=\{(\bm{I}_{s},\mathcal{C}_{s})\}_{s=1}^{S}, where 𝑰s∈ℝH×W×3\bm{I}_{s}\in\mathbb{R}^{H\times W\times 3} is an image, 𝒞s\mathcal{C}_{s} its calibration and each view may come from a different camera model, and a feed-forward network predicts the camera-to-world pose 𝑻s∈S​E​(3)\bm{T}_{s}\in SE(3) of every view and a point for every pixel. The dense head predicts one distance d𝒖d_{\bm{u}} per pixel, read along the pixel’s ray, 𝑿𝒖=d𝒖​𝑹𝒖,\bm{X}_{\bm{u}}=d_{\bm{u}}\,\bm{R}_{\bm{u}}, (2) so one decoder serves every camera, including rays at 90∘90^{\circ} or more from the optical axis. Any3R is trained to read the ray field of each view (Fig. 3). Of the panoramic training samples, half are native panoramas and half are views synthesised by resampling a panorama through a randomly drawn camera, sampled uniformly over eight central models, pinhole, Brown–Conrady, equidistant, Kannala–Brandt, MEI, double sphere, equirectangular and cropped equirectangular, with field of view in [30∘,360∘][30^{\circ},360^{\circ}] and rectilinear models capped below the 180∘180^{\circ} asymptote. A fresh camera is drawn per view, so one sample routinely mixes a narrow rectilinear crop with a wide fisheye and a full sphere, and the view count SS is drawn from {2,…,8}\{2,\dots,8\}. The objective is the pretrained backbone’s own, with camera, depth and point terms as in [57], every term masked by 𝑴\bm{M}, the point weight replaced by the solid-angle weight below and no auxiliary loss. Two of its parts rely on the pixel grid, the rotary positional encoding and the per-pixel training loss, and Any3R gives 𝑹\bm{R} to both, as Fig. 3 shows. MapA-ray is MapAnything given the true ray field, and PaGeR, which predicts no pose, is not shown. 4.1 Setup We train Any3R for 4040 epochs on 3232 GH200 GPUs over about 2727 hours, on Matterport3D [5], Stanford2D3DS [1], Structured3D [79], PanoCity and DL3DV [35] videos, holding out all test scenes. We evaluate on two indoor datasets, Matterport3D and Stanford2D3DS, and one outdoor dataset, PanoCity, with 2424 held-out scenes per dataset and 33 views per scene. Every scene is rendered through 1313 central camera models, the eight used in training and five held out, from the same panoramas and view directions, so two results differ by the lens alone, and Tabs. 1 and 3 report the eight. Every method fuses its views into one point cloud with its own predicted camera poses, and we measure both the cloud and the poses. Accuracy (Acc) is the mean distance from a reconstructed point to its nearest ground-truth point after the similarity alignment and ICP refinement of the PanoVGGT evaluation [18], the point-cloud accuracy used across feed-forward reconstruction [59, 61]. Pose accuracy is the area under the curve (AUC) of the pairwise pose error, the larger of the relative rotation error and the translation-direction error, up to 15∘15^{\circ} and 30∘30^{\circ}, as in [57, 61]. Any3R receives the ray map, MapAnything a pinhole intrinsic matrix on the pinhole cameras, Wid3R the true ray field and the other methods images alone, and MapA-ray is MapAnything given the true ray field on every camera. 4.2 Comparison with the State of the Art Camera-aware methods. Per-view evaluation concerns what the angular coordinates read in a sample whose views come from different cameras. With it, the coordinates of each view are computed from that view’s own ray field, and without it, from the ray field of the first view, which all views of the sample then share. Every test view of Tab. 4 is a panorama, so the two inputs give identical coordinates there, and the difference between the rows was learned in training, where the shared field assigns every view but the first the angles of a lens it was not taken with and the network learns to relate views through wrong angles. Reading each view’s own field gives the largest pose gain on the indoor datasets, and on PanoCity it lowers the median rotation error but also the AUC. On its own, solid-angle weighting gives a smaller gain by counting each direction of the scene once instead of each pixel. The pose gains cost some depth accuracy, most of it with per-view evaluation, which raises the mean log10\log_{10} depth error on Matterport3D from 0.0330.033 to 0.0450.045. We accept this trade for the larger gain in pose. 5 Conclusion Geometry foundation models typically assume pinhole cameras, an assumption embedded in their positional encoding and training objectives. Any3R conditions on dense ray fields through angular coordinates and solid-angle weighting, adapting each camera to the backbone to enable a unified model across central camera types. This feed-forward approach reconstructs scenes from arbitrary combinations of central cameras without calibration search, image undistortion, or lens-specific retraining. Evaluated on thirteen central camera models across three datasets, Any3R achieves state-of-the-art reconstruction accuracy using its own predicted camera poses. Any3R enables scalable 3D reconstruction from heterogeneous image collections, including crowdsourced and archival imagery captured by smartphones, fisheye surveillance cameras, and panoramic imaging systems. Supplementary Material Appendix A Evaluation Details Each of the 2424 scenes of a dataset is a set of three views drawn without replacement, whose view directions are fixed by a pre-registered rule on the 90∘90^{\circ} pinhole and reused for every camera. The thirteen camera models are the eight used in training and five held out from it, FOV/ATAN at 150∘150^{\circ}, stereographic at 180∘180^{\circ}, equisolid and EUCM at 200∘200^{\circ} and cylindrical at 360∘360^{\circ}. Structured3D is evaluated in the same way on all 1616 of its test scenes and reported only in this supplement, since the views of a scene share no content and relative pose cannot be recovered there. Each method’s per-view points are placed with its own predicted poses and fused, and 40,00040{,}000 pixels are sampled per scene. The evaluation function of PanoVGGT then fits one similarity transform on the pixel correspondences between the predicted and the ground-truth points, refines it by point-to-point ICP with a maximum correspondence distance of 0.10.1 m indoors and 1.01.0 m on PanoCity, and reports Acc, the mean distance from a reconstructed point to its nearest ground-truth point, and a cell’s value is the mean over its scenes. The AUC follows the authors’ pose evaluation, taking for every pair of views the larger of the relative rotation error and the translation-direction error, in 1∘1^{\circ} bins, and dropping pairs whose ground-truth translation is below 0.010.01. Baselines with two input options, π3\pi^{3}, VGGT and VGGT-Ω\Omega at their native or their official resolution and MapAnything with or without the pinhole intrinsics, are shown with the option of lower median Acc in each cell, and Tab. 17 lists both. MapAnything accepts a dense ray map, but its released preprocessing fits a pinhole intrinsic matrix to any ray map before cropping, so MapA-ray takes the images through that preprocessing and then receives, for every processed pixel, the true ray of the camera, which enters its ray encoder unchanged. The zero-shot samples of Tab. 2 use the calibrated Kannala–Brandt model of each lens, inverted per pixel, with the images resized to the size of the rendered cameras of similar field of view. On ScanNet++ the views are the DSLR images of every scene of the validation split, the full set with ground truth since the test splits publish no laser scan, posed by its COLMAP reconstruction, which lies in the frame of the laser scan, and the ground truth is the range along each ray to the scanned mesh. On FIORD the views are those of the second lens of the rig, posed by its COLMAP reconstruction brought into the frame of the laser scan by a similarity transform, and the ground truth is the laser cloud z-buffered into each view; the scenes are meetingroom and parakennus, and upstairs is left out because its reconstruction does not register to its scan. In each scene the second view is the first one after an anchor that differs from it by at least 15∘15^{\circ} or 0.30.3 m and sees at least 30%30\% of its ground truth, and the third the first such view before the anchor that also overlaps the second, a rule fixed before any method was run. Wid3R receives the lens through its Fisheye624 class, which contains this model, and the rays it consumes are checked per pixel against the calibrated ones. Where this supplement counts a method as best in a cell, a pre-registered one-sided per-scene sign test also separates it from the runner-up at p<0.05p<0.05. On equirectangular input the fallback and the true ray field coincide up to floating-point rounding, so the two columns are not merely close, they are the same computation, and their Acc agrees to three decimals on all four datasets. Everything the module does elsewhere therefore costs nothing on the input the backbone was pretrained for. Read together with the rest of the figure, the pair says that the gain is not a general improvement to the backbone but a correction that activates exactly where the pretrained assumption stops holding, and is inert where it still holds. Tab. 19 gives the Acc of one checkpoint under three ray maps, and without the ray map its error grows on every dataset. A small CNN trained leave-one-dataset-out to predict each view’s camera type and field of view gives a far more accurate map than the fallback, yet recovers part of the gap only on Matterport3D and is worse than the fallback elsewhere, so the backbone needs an accurate map rather than an approximate one. The decoder places each point along its ray, so a direction error moves a point in proportion to its distance, which is largest on PanoCity, whose outdoor scenes the predictor never saw, and the predictor also replaces the exact map of the equirectangular frames, where the fallback needed no correction. Fig. 10 shows two of them on the same frames. With no calibration the backbone falls back to assuming an equirectangular grid, so a 90∘90^{\circ} pinhole image is treated as a full sphere, and the geometry is wrong over most of the frame. The bottom row is the internal control: on equirectangular input the fallback is the correct ray map, and the two columns are indistinguishable. That row is the exactness constraint of Sec. 3.2 made visible. Figure 10: One checkpoint with and without the ray map. Appendix H Accuracy Against Field of View Normalising each method by its own 90∘90^{\circ} value is what makes the comparison about the lens rather than about the method’s overall quality. A method that is uniformly weak but equally weak everywhere traces a flat line; a method that depends on the projection traces a rising one. Fig. 13 plots the five field-of-view bands of Tab. 15 with each method divided by its own Acc at 90∘90^{\circ} and averaged over the three datasets of the paired pool, so the curves start together and only the slope is compared. A value below one means the method is more accurate on the wider lens than on the narrow one it was measured against. Any3R stays below one at every wider field of view, because the encoding and the objective both read the measured ray field, so a wider lens is simply more of the scene per view. PanoVGGT stays close to one and dips below it everywhere except at 180∘180^{\circ}, because its fixed equirectangular assumption is satisfied at the wide end and violated at the narrow end, which is the same failure as the perspective models with the axis reversed. Every perspective-native method rises above one in every band. Figure 13: Acc against field of view, each method normalised by its own 90∘90^{\circ} value and averaged over the three datasets. Appendix I Real Pinhole Imagery Every camera in the benchmark is rendered from a panorama, so a reader may reasonably ask whether the model has learnt something about resampled imagery rather than about cameras. Tab. 20 therefore evaluates camera pose on DL3DV [35], a capture set of real phone and camera video with no panoramic origin and no resampling step, over 6060 scenes at three views each. On a pinhole the upper half of the rotary frequencies still spans the image as the stock encoding does, so the mechanism is meant to leave this regime close to the pretrained one. Our mean over scenes of the per-scene median rotation error is the lowest of the seven methods and our AUC at 30∘30^{\circ} the second highest, while DA3 leads the AUC at 5∘5^{\circ}. Any3R is thus at the level of VGGT on every measure, so the pinhole prior survives. PanoVGGT, trained on panoramas alone, makes the mirror error and reads every photograph as a panorama, so its rotation error is far larger than that of any other method.
