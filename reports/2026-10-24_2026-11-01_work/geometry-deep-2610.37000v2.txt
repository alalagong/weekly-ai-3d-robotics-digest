However, VGGT’s global attention is quadratic in the number of frames and dominates end-to-end runtime on long sequences, which has motivated a range of training-free efficiency methods such as token merging. These methods, however, come with a drop in accuracy that makes them hard to use in place of VGGT. We propose GPA-VGGT, a training-free acceleration method that replaces global attention in early blocks with frame attention augmented by a shared cross-frame K/V set. In the remaining blocks, we merge only keys and values while preserving all queries, restore the attention mass of the tokens each merged key represents, and randomly project the matching metric to 128 dimensions to reduce matching cost. Across diverse multi-view geometry tasks, GPA-VGGT provides training-free acceleration at speedups comparable to prior work while preserving the information required for precise geometric estimation, retaining over 99%99\% of VGGT’s pose accuracy under AUC@3. Our project page is available at https://noheat61.github.io/GPA-VGGT/. 1 Introduction After pairwise systems such as DUSt3R [11], feed-forward models including Fast3R [12], π3\pi^{3} [13], and VGGT [1] extended direct 3D reconstruction to multi-view inputs and have become widely used backbones for multi-view 3D perception. VGGT jointly predicts camera parameters, depth maps, point maps, and tracks from one to hundreds of unposed views. However, this accuracy comes at a substantial computational cost: its aggregator alternates between frame attention, whose complexity scales linearly with the number of views, and global attention, whose complexity scales quadratically. On long sequences, global attention dominates end-to-end runtime and becomes the primary bottleneck, limiting the scalability of VGGT to the many-view settings it was designed to support. Figure 1: Accuracy and runtime scaling with the number of input frames. Although existing acceleration methods substantially reduce end-to-end runtime, they incur pronounced camera-pose degradation under the strict AUC@3 metric. GPA-VGGT achieves comparable runtime while maintaining camera-pose accuracy close to that of vanilla VGGT. This bottleneck has motivated a growing body of acceleration methods. FastVGGT [2] introduces Token Merging (ToMe) [15] into the aggregator, whereas FasterVGGT [6] replaces dense global attention with block-sparse attention. AVGGT [3] replaces early global-attention blocks with frame attention and subsamples keys in later blocks, while LiteVGGT [4] uses geometry-aware cached token merging and FlashVGGT [5] compresses the global aggregator with learned descriptors, both relying on fine-tuning for acceleration. Although these methods report substantial speedups with little loss in accuracy, evaluation under a common protocol shows that this accuracy preservation is largely confined to lenient metrics. At relaxed pose thresholds (e.g., AUC@3030), the degradation may appear minor; however, as Figure 1 shows, camera estimates become noticeably less precise under strict thresholds (e.g., AUC@33). The former primarily reflects coarse pose correctness and can mask errors that remain consequential for reconstruction, whereas the latter directly tests the geometric precision required for an accelerated method to serve as a practical replacement for VGGT. We introduce GPA-VGGT, a training-free acceleration method designed to preserve the information required for accurate geometric estimation. GPA-VGGT merges keys and values only, retaining every query so that each token continues to produce its own output. It further adds log⁡s\log s to the logit of each merged or pooled key, restoring the attention mass of the tokens it represents. In early blocks, GPA-VGGT preserves cross-frame context through a compact shared cross-frame K/V set comprising the camera and register tokens from all frames, together with a small number of content-adaptive pooled patch summaries. Finally, because the retained similarity is used only for an argmax operation, we reduce matching cost through a fixed low-dimensional projection of the matching metric. Without additional training, GPA-VGGT processes a 200-frame sequence in approximately 3434 s, compared with 8787 s for vanilla VGGT, while matching the runtime of other baselines. Across all benchmarks evaluated using AUC@3, GPA-VGGT remains within 1%1\% of vanilla VGGT on benchmarks and shows a particularly large advantage over existing acceleration methods on the more challenging setting. Our contributions are summarized below: • We analyze accuracy degradation in existing VGGT acceleration methods, including FastVGGT and AVGGT, and identify three distinct sources: query merging, attention-mass distortion, and early global to frame attention replacement. • We combine Pooled Cross-Frame Broadcast to retain early cross-frame context with Query-Preserving K/V Merging to compress later global attention while retaining every query token, and use low-dimensional matching to reduce overhead. • We evaluate GPA-VGGT on representative benchmarks, maintaining strong performance across multi-view geometry tasks without fine-tuning. 2 Related Work 2.1 VGGT Acceleration VGGT [1] is a feed-forward multi-view geometry model that takes a set of unposed images as input and jointly predicts camera parameters, depth maps, point maps, and tracking features for all views. Each view is tokenized by a DINOv2 [24] encoder, and its Transformer [25] alternates between frame attention and global attention. Although global attention is essential for establishing multi-view consistency, its quadratic complexity with respect to the number of tokens makes it the primary computational bottleneck for long image sequences. FlashVGGT [5] compresses the global aggregator using learned descriptors. LiteVGGT [4] introduces geometry-aware cached token merging, preserving geometrically important tokens while reusing merge indices across adjacent blocks. Co-Me [9] uses an auxiliary confidence predictor, trained by distillation, to identify tokens suitable for merging. QuantVGGT [7] and QVGGT [8] improve computational and memory efficiency through reduced-precision inference. Because these methods rely on additional training or reduced precision, they are not the target of our training-free architectural design. FastVGGT [2] was the first method to introduce token merging into VGGT. To address token collapse in long sequences, it uses a partitioning strategy tailored to the multi-frame layout and preserves a subset of salient tokens from merging. FasterVGGT [6] replaces dense global attention with training-free block-sparse attention, retaining selected block interactions based on coarse attention patterns and geometrically meaningful correspondences. AVGGT [3] replaces early global blocks with frame attention and applies uniform grid-based subsampling to patch tokens in the remaining global blocks. Its design is motivated by the observation that global attention supports cross-view alignment through sparse spatial correspondences rather than dense token-to-token interactions. HTTM [10] performs head-wise temporal token merging with temporal reordering to preserve feature diversity after head concatenation. Although these methods provide substantial acceleration, their accuracy degradation becomes more apparent under strict metrics such as AUC@3, which are sensitive to fine-grained camera-pose errors. Our method instead preserves the information required for precise geometric estimation and pose accuracy at AUC@3. 2.2 Token Merging ToMe [15] was first introduced as a training-free approach to improve the throughput of vision transformers, and was later extended to diffusion, video, and language models. ToMe partitions tokens in each block into source and destination sets and merges tokens based on the similarity of their attention keys. Since a merged key represents multiple tokens, ToMe adds the logarithm of the group size to the attention logit so that the merged key receives their combined attention mass. ToMeSD [16] adapts this procedure to Stable Diffusion by selecting destination tokens from a fixed 2D grid and computing similarity from the block input rather than attention keys. It also omits this correction and uses a merge–unmerge scheme to preserve dense outputs. FastVGGT adopts this ToMeSD-style token merging for a 3D geometry backbone, where such design choices can lead to fine-grained information loss and accuracy degradation in precise geometry estimation. 3 Analysis 3.1 Token Merging Disrupts Token-Specific Predictions and Attention Mass FastVGGT [2] merges queries together with keys and values, then restores the original sequence length by assigning each merged output to all tokens in its group. Consequently, non-representative tokens receive outputs that were not computed from their individual queries. This differs fundamentally from an imperfect approximation that can be corrected after merging. FastVGGT preserves salient tokens from merging, but this only partially mitigates the loss of token-specific predictions. Figure 2 (top) plots the member-token outputs associated with twelve representative merge slots selected from block 12, projected onto the first two principal components of the unmerged output field. Query merging collapses within-group output diversity to zero, whereas K/V-only merging closely overlaps with the unmerged outputs in this diagnostic. Figure 2: Two sources of accuracy degradation in existing token-merging methods. Top: Query merging collapses the block-12 outputs of 8383 tokens into 1212 shared outputs, whereas retaining all queries while merging only K/V preserves the output diversity of the unmerged model. Bottom: Omitting the +log⁡s+\log s correction disproportionately allocates attention to the first frame. Restoring the correction recovers an attention distribution close to that of the unmerged model. The number of original tokens represented by each merged key, its token size ss, is accounted for by adding +log⁡s+\log s to the corresponding attention logit. Following ToMeSD [16], FastVGGT omits this correction, so a merged key receives the mass of only a single token. In VGGT, this omission introduces an asymmetric bias because the first frame consists predominantly of destination tokens; attention is therefore progressively biased toward the first frame across merged blocks. Figure 2 (bottom) shows that, without the correction, the first frame receives nearly twice as much attention mass as in the unmerged model, whereas applying the correction restores a distribution close to the unmerged one. This effect causes a larger accuracy loss than query merging, and the losses from the two factors are approximately additive. 3.2 Early Blocks Still Need Cross-Frame Context AVGGT’s layer-wise analysis [3] concludes that early global-attention blocks do not form meaningful cross-view correspondences, motivating their conversion to frame attention. This finding supports replacing dense global attention, but does not establish that early blocks can dispense with cross-frame context entirely. The absence of token-level correspondences does not preclude informative interactions at the regional level, where compact cross-frame context can still support geometric estimation. Figure 3 demonstrates that the principal limitation of early frame attention is not merely representational drift, but the disruption of a shared geometric reference across views. In the top row of Figure 3, frame attention displaces the block-8 output field by 15.5%15.5\% of its radius relative to the full-global-attention reference, whereas the shared cross-frame K/V set reduces this displacement to 7.8%7.8\%. The bottom row further reveals that this drift is view-specific rather than globally shared: the pairwise cosine similarities between per-frame drift directions exhibit a broad distribution, with some views moving coherently and others in opposing directions. Their mean similarity is −0.09-0.09, close to zero, indicating the absence of a common drift direction. Whereas a common drift preserves relative geometry, such inconsistent view-specific perturbations alter inter-view relationships and degrade relative-pose estimation. Adding shared special tokens and pooled patch summaries progressively aligns the per-frame drifts, thereby preserving the shared geometric reference. Early blocks require cross-frame context because frame attention induces view-specific representational drift, disrupting the relative geometry required for pose estimation. Preserving this shared geometric reference, however, does not require the full cross-frame key set; a compact shared context is sufficient. Incorporating cross-frame special tokens partially mitigates the drift, and augmenting them with image-token summaries yields further improvement. 4 GPA-VGGT 4.1 Query-Preserving K/V Merging with Token-Size Compensation We merge keys and values while preserving the full query sequence. Each token therefore attends to the reduced key set using its own query and retains an individual output. In contrast to FastVGGT [2], which computes attention over Nmerged×NmergedN_{\text{merged}}\times N_{\text{merged}} tokens, our formulation has complexity N×NmergedN\times N_{\text{merged}}. This increases the attention cost by a factor of N/NmergedN/N_{\text{merged}} relative to merging queries, but avoids the accuracy loss caused by replacing token-specific outputs with shared ones. For the remaining configurations, we follow FastVGGT, using the same grouping rule and merge ratio of 0.90.9. After K/V merging, the remaining approximation is the replacement of ss keys by their mean. The merged key then has token size ss but receives the attention mass of a single key. We correct this bias by adding log⁡s\log s to the attention logit of each merged key, without altering the merged key vector. Each query attends to three disjoint groups: patch tokens from its own frame, the camera and four register tokens from all frames, and pooled patch summaries from all frames. Pooling keeps the shared set compact, while including each frame’s own summaries makes the set identical across frames. It can therefore be constructed once and reused without masks or per-frame K/V copies. For frame ff, the key set is 𝒦f=[Kfpatch;K1:Fspecial;K¯1:Fpool]\mathcal{K}_{f}=[K^{\text{patch}}_{f};\,K^{\text{special}}_{1:F};\,\bar{K}^{\text{pool}}_{1:F}], and the value set is built in the same way. We construct content-adaptive pooled summaries using cluster-h. We initialize representative locations on a stride-88 grid, assign each patch token to the representative with the highest cosine similarity, and average the assigned keys and values to form each summary. Unlike fixed-window average pooling, this groups patches with similar content rather than merely nearby locations. Since multi-head attention splits each 10241024-dimensional token into 1616 heads of dimension 6464, cluster-h performs this assignment independently for each head using its head-specific features. Thus, a patch can be grouped differently across heads according to the information each head captures. Each pooled summary represents ss patch tokens but enters attention as a single key, so we apply the same +log⁡s+\log s correction. This sensitivity follows from the attention patterns. In the middle blocks, queries attend sharply to a small number of keys, particularly cross-view correspondences; averaging keys can therefore dilute the information carried by the most relevant key. In later blocks, attention is more diffuse and primarily directed toward global sink tokens, making merging less disruptive. We consequently use a merge ratio of 0.20.2 for blocks 1313–1616 and retain the ratio of 0.90.9 for all other blocks in the merging window. 4.4 Gaussian Random Projection for Efficient Matching Token matching incurs additional overhead because it determines the source–destination assignments before merging. Assigning each source token to its most similar destination requires an O⁡(src×dst×C)O(\text{src}\times\text{dst}\times C) argmax operation, where CC denotes the feature dimension. In the accelerated model with the preceding components applied, matching accounts for approximately 18%18\% of the forward-pass time for a 200200-frame sequence when using the full C=1024C=1024 representation. We reduce matching cost by projecting the matching features into a lower-dimensional space using a random Gaussian matrix and performing cosine normalization and argmax in that space. Each source token ii is assigned to the destination d⁡(i)=arg⁡maxj∈𝒟⁡cos⁡(P⊤​xi,P⊤​xj),d(i)=\arg\max_{j\in\mathcal{D}}\,\cos\!\big(P^{\top}x_{i},\,P^{\top}x_{j}\big), (2) where xi∈ℝ1024x_{i}\in\mathbb{R}^{1024} is its matching feature, 𝒟\mathcal{D} the destination set, and P∈ℝ1024×128P\in\mathbb{R}^{1024\times 128} a random Gaussian matrix shared across all blocks and inputs. Reducing the feature dimension from 10241024 to 128128 lowers the cost of matching by approximately 77–8×8\times. The similarity scores are used only to select destination indices and are discarded thereafter; merging averages the tokens assigned to each group. Even when projection changes the selected destination, it typically selects an alternative among near-tied candidates: token features are highly redundant, and the most similar destination is rarely unique. Thus, random projection may reorder nearly equivalent matches without producing a fundamentally different assignment. Since merging requires each token to be grouped with a sufficiently similar token rather than one specific destination, the projection reduces matching cost while preserving similarity-based token groups. 4.5 Custom Kernels Two custom Triton [22] kernels implement the design: a FlashAttention [21]-style kernel that adds the +log⁡s+\log s bias inside its online-softmax loop, since passing it as a mask makes PyTorch [23] SDPA fall back to a slower backend, and a matching kernel that computes the source–destination argmax without storing the score matrix. Each row adds one component to the row above it. 6 Conclusion We identify three sources of accuracy degradation in accelerated VGGT: information loss from query merging, incorrect attention mass after K/V merging, and the removal of early cross-frame context. GPA-VGGT addresses these issues through Query-Preserving K/V Merging with +log⁡s+\log s correction, Pooled Cross-Frame Broadcast, and low-dimensional matching. Across diverse multi-view geometry evaluations, GPA-VGGT reduces inference time while preserving vanilla VGGT’s accuracy under both standard and strict metrics. In NeurIPS, 2017. \thetitle Supplementary Material Appendix A Custom Triton Kernels A.1 Attention with a Per-Key Additive Bias The standard attention interface treats the +log⁡s+\log s correction as a mask and therefore falls back to a slower implementation. For a 200200-frame sequence, this fallback takes 2,1422{,}142 ms, compared with 948948 ms for unbiased fused attention—more time than key merging saves in the merged global-attention block. Because the correction depends only on the key index, our Triton kernel adds it directly to each score tile inside the online-softmax loop, preserving the streaming structure of FlashAttention [21]. We avoid masked loads by clamping out-of-range key indices and assigning duplicate columns a −109-10^{9} bias. This avoids padding and copying K/V tensors; only the small bias vector is padded. The bias is also pre-scaled on the host, avoiding conversion within the inner loop. For a 200200-frame sequence, our kernel takes 984984 ms: only 4%4\% slower than unbiased fused attention and 2.18×2.18\times faster than the fallback. A.2 Memory-Efficient K/V Matching Bipartite matching requires only the row-wise maximum similarity and argmax index, not the full similarity matrix. Materializing the full matrix would require 27.2527.25 GiB for a 200200-frame sequence. Even a chunked PyTorch [23] reference, which materializes 5,0005{,}000 rows at a time, requires a 1.201.20 GiB temporary buffer. Our Triton kernel keeps each score tile in registers and writes only the two required outputs, reducing this temporary memory to 0.0030.003 GiB. The kernel trades a modest increase in runtime for substantially lower memory use: the similarity kernel itself takes 482482 ms against 330330 ms for the chunked reference at this shape. This overhead of roughly 150150 ms is small relative to the memory saved, since materializing similarity tiles incurs a large transient VRAM cost and can become the limiting factor for long sequences. The PyTorch reference delegates the 1,0241{,}024-dimensional dot-product reduction to its matrix-multiplication backend. In our fused kernel, which additionally computes the row-wise maximum and argmax, we partition the feature dimension into smaller tiles and accumulate partial dot products. Appendix D Merge-Operator Ablation The merge operator independently determines whether to merge queries with K/V tokens and whether to correct each merged key’s logit by its token size. Table S3 isolates these choices under an otherwise fixed configuration. Merging queries forces all tokens within a group to share the same output, whereas preserving queries retains within-group diversity but does not address the attention-mass bias caused by K/V merging. The +log⁡s+\log s correction restores the mass of the tokens each merged key represents and provides the larger accuracy gain. Together, query preservation and the +log⁡s+\log s correction nearly recover no-merging accuracy, with the benefit becoming larger at higher merge ratios. Appendix E Selection of the Core Blocks Figure S1: Core-block selection on VGGT. Each point shows how much the final global-block output changes from the unmerged model when the indicated contiguous block set is protected from merging; lower values mean smaller changes. The criterion is computed without ground-truth annotations across four datasets. Blocks 1313–1616 consistently give the smallest change and are selected as the core-block set. We choose the core blocks by measuring how much merging changes the aggregator’s final representation. For each candidate set, we leave those blocks unmerged and apply merging to every other block. We measure the relative Frobenius-norm deviation between the final global-block outputs of the merged and unmerged models on the special tokens, and normalize it by the deviation obtained when all blocks are merged. Because this measurement uses no ground-truth annotations, the core blocks are selected without reference to task labels. We evaluate block sets rather than individual blocks. Simply ranking individual blocks and selecting the top kk does not reliably find the best set. A single-block measurement considers one block while all other blocks remain unmerged, whereas the full model applies merging across multiple blocks. Merging in one block can therefore change the token representations and source–destination assignments used by later blocks. For VGGT, this criterion selects blocks 1313–1616. Figure S1 shows that this set gives the smallest output change on all four datasets. No three-block set matches this result. We apply the same procedure to π3\pi^{3} and VGGT-Ω\Omega; Figure S2 shows the resulting core-block selections, which are used in Section H. Appendix G Comparison with AVGGT GPA-VGGT and AVGGT [3] share a two-stage structure: both replace early global-attention blocks with frame attention and reduce the K/V set in later blocks while preserving all queries. In the early stage, AVGGT restricts attention to within-frame tokens, whereas GPA-VGGT augments each frame with a pooled shared cross-frame K/V set. In the later stage, AVGGT retains K/V tokens at fixed spatial strides, whereas GPA-VGGT selects them through bipartite merging. At an equal token budget, AVGGT summarizes all removed tokens with a single unweighted mean K/V pair, whereas GPA-VGGT aggregates each removed token into the retained token it most closely matches and restores the corresponding attention mass through the +log⁡s+\log s correction. The 128128-dimensional Gaussian projection reduces matching overhead, enabling GPA-VGGT to retain fewer K/V tokens than AVGGT(2) while achieving both lower runtime and higher accuracy. Appendix H Transfer to Other Backbones The proposed design applies to backbones that alternate frame attention and global attention over a token layout comprising special tokens followed by a patch grid. Both π3\pi^{3} [13] and VGGT-Ω\Omega [14] follow this structure. We adjust only model-specific properties, including the block layout, the number of special tokens per frame, and patch size. Each adaptation uses the same 128128-dimensional Gaussian projection for K/V matching. Table S5 reports the incremental ablation on π3\pi^{3}. We retain the early-block setting used by AVGGT: global attention in blocks 00–99 is replaced by frame attention, and K/V merging is applied in blocks 1010–1717, with a lower merge ratio in core blocks 1313–1515. However, π3\pi^{3} requires a lower overall merge ratio than VGGT because it has fewer global-attention blocks. Each merged block therefore has a larger effect on the information propagated through the remaining global-attention blocks. Table S6 reports the same ablation on VGGT-Ω\Omega, which contains five register blocks at raw indices 22, 66, 99, 1414, and 2020 that do not perform cross-frame attention over patch tokens. We therefore replace global attention with frame attention in raw blocks 00, 11, 33, 44, 55, 77, and 88, and apply K/V merging in the subsequent non-register blocks, with a lower merge ratio in raw blocks 1212, 1313, 1515, and 1616. As in π3\pi^{3}, patch tokens participate in global attention in fewer blocks than in VGGT, requiring a lower overall merge ratio. On both backbones, the shared cross-frame K/V set mitigates the accuracy loss caused by replacing early global attention with frame attention. The adopted configurations accelerate all three backbones while retaining most of the accuracy of their vanilla models.
