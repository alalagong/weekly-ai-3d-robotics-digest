TL;DR Any3R is a feed-forward 3D reconstruction model that works with any central camera, including fisheyes beyond 180°, 360° panoramas and any mix of lenses in one scene, because it reads each view's ray field instead of assuming a pinhole. Abstract Geometry foundation models recover camera poses and dense geometry from a set of images with a feed-forward network, but they expect pinhole images. That assumption is inside the backbone too: the positional encoding and the training loss both bind an image position to a direction in space, and both depend on the lens. We condition both on the dense ray field, the unit direction through every pixel, which describes any central camera without a lens type, distortion coefficients or a bound on field of view. Angular coordinates give the rotary encoding the measured ray angle of each patch, evaluated per view, and solid-angle weighting scales the point loss by the area each pixel covers on the sphere, so neither the encoding nor the supervision depends on the lens. Any3R is therefore a feed-forward model that takes any central camera, and is robust to mixing many camera types per scene, with no retraining per lens. On a benchmark of thirteen central cameras across three datasets, where every method reconstructs with its own predicted camera poses, it is the most consistently accurate method. Any3R enables scalable 3D reconstruction from heterogeneous crowdsourced and archival imagery collections sourced across diverse camera types. How it works A central camera is fully described by the direction of the ray through each pixel, whatever the lens. Any3R takes this ray field with every view and uses it in the two places where a pinhole model silently assumes a lens. Ray field in, any lensEach view comes with the unit ray of every pixel, computed from any calibration: pinhole, distortion polynomials, fisheye models or panoramas. Views with different cameras and sizes share one forward pass. Angular coordinatesThe rotary position embedding locates each patch by the measured elevation and azimuth of its rays, per view, instead of its row and column, so attention sees where a patch looks rather than where it sits in the image. Solid-angle weightingThe point loss is weighted by the area each pixel covers on the sphere, so every direction counts by how much of the world it covers, not by how many pixels a lens happens to spend on it.
